\begin{abstract}
The continuous routing of autonomous service fleets—such as retail shopping carts (\textit{Int-Cart}), clinical hospital pushchairs, and airport luggage trolleys—in crowded, human-shared public environments poses fundamental multi-agent coordination challenges. Classical shortest-path and reactive collision-avoidance methods treat pedestrians as obstacles to be cleared rather than as people whose personal space carries a cost, and they possess no mechanism for resolving symmetric conflicts in single-file corridors. This paper proposes the Distributed Dynamic Route Optimization ($\text{D}^2\text{RO}$) framework powered by Socially-Weighted Distributed Graph Optimization (SW-DGO). $\text{D}^2\text{RO}$ formalizes a dimensionally weighted edge traversal cost, $C_i(e, t) = w_D \cdot C_{\text{geom}}(e) + w_M \cdot C_{\text{mesh}}(e, t) + w_H \cdot C_{\text{social}}(e, t) + w_S \cdot C_{\text{kinematic}}(i, e, t)$, minimised \emph{subject to} a directional corridor reservation constraint ($R_{\text{lock}}$), and solved by incremental heuristic graph repair ($\text{D}^*$ Lite) over event-driven Vehicle-to-Vehicle (V2V) telemetry with exponential decay, continuous 2D asymmetric Gaussian human proxemics, and non-holonomic clearance envelopes. We evaluate over $4{,}600$ kinodynamic simulation trials in eleven experiments, designed so that each claim is attributable. A $2\times2$ factorial isolates social \emph{routing} ($H_{\text{prox}}$) from reactive human \emph{yielding} under dynamic $\text{D}^*$ Lite search, showing that routing alone reduces intimate-space exposure from $6.45$ to $0.00$ person-seconds ($95\%$ CI $[-6.48, -6.31]$) while yielding contributes nothing on a social route and collapses success on a frozen one. Against socially blind planners the effect is categorical: median exposure $0$ person-seconds [IQR $0$, $0$] versus $6.40$ [$6.2$, $6.6$] for a matched-controller shortest path ($N=100$ seed-paired trials, Holm-adjusted $p<10^{-15}$), bought with makespan of $47.18 \pm 13.40\text{ s}$ against $19.20\text{ s}$. We also report a negative result that bounds the contribution honestly: an ordinary human-aware planner without any distributed layer matches that social compliance exactly ($p=1$) while running $8\text{ s}$ faster, so the mesh and reservation earn their cost only under the topologies our controlled experiments isolate --- anticipatory rerouting $11.10\text{ s}$ before a blockage becomes observable, and corridor success raised from $36\%$ to $88\%$ by cost-projected diversion. Sensitivity over the four weighted terms holds success at $97$--$100\%$ throughout; anticipation degrades measurably beyond $10\%$ packet loss. The unchanged planner transfers across supermarket, hospital and airport topologies ($99.0\%$, $100.0\%$, $95.0\%$) with $\text{D}^*$ Lite repairs costing a median $0.002\text{ ms}$ within a $0.12\text{ ms}$ control step.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{T}{he} rapid advancement of autonomous mobile robots (AMRs), ubiquitous sensor networks, and edge computing has catalyzed a paradigm shift in service robotics. While early automated guided vehicles (AGVs) operated primarily within segregated industrial warehouses—isolated from humans behind physical safety barriers and governed by rigid guide-paths—the next generation of autonomous service fleets must operate directly within \textbf{human-shared, highly dynamic, and unstructured public environments} \cite{azlan2024intcart, kruse2013human, chen2020relational}.

Such fleets already appear in several settings. Smart retail shopping trolleys (\textit{Int-Cart}) escort shoppers, assist those with mobility impairments, collect items for online grocery fulfilment, and return themselves from checkout to front-of-store depots \cite{azlan2024intcart}. Clinical pushchairs and mobile beds carry patients and supplies between emergency departments, operating suites, radiology, and inpatient wards. Airport luggage trolleys move heavy baggage across open departure concourses, through security screening, and along narrow boarding piers. Library and micro-fulfilment AGVs work in single-file book stacks and storage aisles alongside staff and patrons. What unites them is not the payload but the setting: each shares its floor with people who are under no obligation to get out of the way.

Unlike static industrial warehouses where aisles are wide and traffic flows are centralized, public service environments present unique, compound multi-agent challenges. In these settings, space is severely constrained by orthogonal shelving fixtures, corridors are frequently single-file, and human pedestrians move stochastically with complex browsing and loitering behaviors \cite{kruse2013human}.

\subsection{Core Research Motivations \& Technical Bottlenecks}
When deployed in complex, fixture-dense public service environments, traditional Multi-Agent Path Finding (MAPF) and reactive obstacle avoidance algorithms suffer from four fundamental technical bottlenecks:

\subsubsection{The Geometry-Kinematic Disconnect in Concave Fixtures (The ORCA Trap)}
\label{sec:orca_trap}
Classical continuous collision avoidance methods—such as Optimal Reciprocal Collision Avoidance (ORCA) \cite{vandenberg2008orca} and Artificial Potential Fields (APF)—rely on local velocity-space half-planes or repulsive force vectors. In open spaces, these methods produce smooth, collision-free trajectories. 

However, in realistic retail and hospital layouts filled with orthogonal $90^\circ$ shelf corners, end-cap displays, and narrow U-bays, reactive methods are prone to \textbf{local potential minima}. When a robot encounters a pedestrian near a shelf corner, the repulsive force from the human and the repulsive vector from the orthogonal wall can cancel the goal attraction vector ($\|\mathbf{F}_{\text{net}}\| \to 0$), stalling the agent until the pedestrian moves on.

We stress that this is a \emph{delay} mechanism, not necessarily a failure mechanism. Our measurements (Section~\ref{sec:failure_modes}) show that, given a time budget calibrated to the true mission duration, an artificial potential field completes every mission it is given; what it cannot do is complete them without repeatedly driving through people. The deficiency of reactive avoidance in these settings is therefore social and temporal rather than a simple inability to reach the goal, and the contribution of this paper should be read against that corrected baseline.

\subsubsection{Social Blindness of Static Shortest-Path Solvers (The \texorpdfstring{$A^*$}{A*} Flaw)}
Traditional discrete graph planners (e.g., Static $A^*$, Dijkstra) compute global paths based strictly on static Euclidean distance $D(u, v)$ \cite{stern2019multi}. While computationally straightforward, static solvers are socially blind: they treat human pedestrians either as non-existent or as infinitesimal points. When a corridor becomes crowded with browsing shoppers or medical staff, a static planner relentlessly commands the robot to drive straight through the crowd, inducing severe psychological discomfort and intimate personal space violations ($d < 0.8\text{ m}$).

\subsubsection{Symmetrical Live-Locks in Single-File Corridors}
In narrow architectural passages (e.g., supermarket grocery aisles, clinical hospital corridors) whose width does not admit two carts side by side with the required clearances (Eq.~\eqref{eq:singlefile}), bidirectional passing is not possible. When two opposing agents enter a single-file aisle simultaneously, reactive avoidance algorithms oscillate in place, producing permanent symmetrical live-locks \cite{dergachev2021distributed}.

\subsubsection{Information Isolation and Delayed Fleet Backtracking}
In decentralized multi-agent systems without communication, robots operate with myopic, onboard sensing radius. When a lead cart encounters an unexpected aisle blockage, trailing carts remain unaware of the obstruction. Trailing units travel all the way to the blocked entrance before sensing the congestion locally, forcing complete reversals and causing severe inflation in fleet makespan \cite{gielis2022codesign}.

\subsection{Target Application Fields}
The framework targets the same four domains, each of which stresses it differently. Retail supermarkets combine narrow grocery aisles with arterial promenades (\textbf{Action Alley}), end-caps and checkout queues. Clinical facilities impose a priority hierarchy: emergency trauma vehicles hold right-of-way while routine pushchairs yield into \textbf{Turnout Alcoves} ($V_{\text{alcove}}$). Aviation terminals pair wide open concourses with security pinch points and elongated gate piers. Public facilities and micro-fulfilment stacks offer the tightest physical clearances of all. Three of these---supermarket, hospital and airport---are implemented as simulation domains and evaluated in Section~\ref{sec:cross_domain_failure}.

\subsection{The Proposed Solution: The \texorpdfstring{$\text{D}^2\text{RO}$}{D2RO} Framework}
To overcome these bottlenecks, this paper proposes the \textbf{Distributed Dynamic Route Optimization (\texorpdfstring{$\text{D}^2\text{RO}$}{D2RO})} framework powered by \textbf{Socially-Weighted Distributed Graph Optimization (SW-DGO)}. Rather than treating navigation as isolated reactive avoidance or purely local search, $\text{D}^2\text{RO}$ establishes a \textbf{distributed, time-varying graph-cost field} $C_i(e, t)$ shared across peer agents:
\begin{multline}
\label{eq:sw_dgo_master_intro}
C_i(e, t) = w_D \cdot C_{\text{geom}}(e) + w_M \cdot C_{\text{mesh}}(e, t) + w_H \cdot C_{\text{social}}(e, t) \\
+ w_S \cdot C_{\text{kinematic}}(i, e, t) \quad \text{subject to } R_{\text{lock}}
\end{multline}
seamlessly unifying incremental heuristic graph repair ($\text{D}^*$ Lite) \cite{koenig2002dstar} with distributed anticipatory cost propagation, continuous Gaussian proxemics \cite{kruse2013human, chen2020relational}, directional corridor reservation protocols ($R_{\text{lock}}$), and non-holonomic vehicle clearance envelopes.

\subsection{Research Aims \& Key Contributions}
\label{sec:aims}
The framework is designed against four explicit aims, and we return to each of them in the discussion and conclusion. \textbf{A1, social compliance:} route service fleets so that pedestrians' intimate space is not entered, accepting bounded additional travel time as the price (Section~\ref{sec:tradeoff}). \textbf{A2, distributed coordination without a central server:} resolve congestion and single-file conflicts using only peer-to-peer information within a bounded sensing radius (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}). \textbf{A3, architectural generality and real-time feasibility:} operate unchanged across dissimilar public topologies within a $50\text{ ms}$ control budget (Sections~\ref{sec:cross_domain_failure} and~\ref{sec:scalability}). \textbf{A4, attributable evaluation:} measure the contribution of the routing policy \emph{itself}, separately from the vehicle controller that executes it, and establish whether the result depends on the particular weights chosen (Sections~\ref{sec:tradeoff} and~\ref{sec:sensitivity}).

The primary scientific contributions of this research are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Distributed Anticipatory Edge-Cost Propagation \& Horizon Extension:} We formalize a distributed dynamic graph-cost field in which localized physical and social perturbations observed by leading agents become peer-to-peer, time-decayed edge penalties $C_{\text{mesh}}(e, t)$. This extends each robot's effective planning horizon ($\mathcal{O}_i^{\text{effective}}(t)$) beyond its onboard line-of-sight sensing radius ($R_s$). Under a controlled experiment that isolates the mechanism (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}), agents divert $10.70 \pm 4.20\text{ s}$ before they could have observed the obstruction directly, and more than halve their backtracking distance ($1.08 \pm 0.68\text{ m}$ vs. $2.73 \pm 0.87\text{ m}$, Holm-adjusted $p = 3.2 \times 10^{-8}$).
    \item \textbf{Distributed Directional Bottleneck Reservation Protocol:} We formalize an explicit distributed protocol for single-file topological bottlenecks (Eq.~\eqref{eq:singlefile}) via directional reservation tuples $\mathcal{L}_e = \langle \text{owner}, \vec{d}, t_{\text{acquire}}, t_{\text{expire}}, \text{priority} \rangle$ and Turnout Alcoves ($V_{\text{alcove}}$). In the controlled corridor experiment the protocol raises mission success from $36.0\%$ to $88.0\%$ ($p = 1.7 \times 10^{-4}$). The mechanism operates by \emph{cost-projected diversion} rather than by mutual exclusion: agents leave the contested corridor ($2.16 \pm 1.36$ off-corridor vertices against $0.00$, $p = 2.3 \times 10^{-8}$) while accumulating $0.030 \pm 0.070\text{ s}$ of waiting. We therefore describe it as a directional reservation rather than a mutex, and analyse the distinction in Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}.
    \item \textbf{Kinodynamically \& Socially Conditioned Incremental Optimization:} We unify asymmetric human proxemic discomfort fields ($C_{\text{social}}$) and vehicle-specific clearance envelopes ($C_{\text{kinematic}}$) directly into an incremental $\text{D}^*$ Lite graph repair engine, executing incremental repairs at a median of $0.002\text{ ms}$ (p95 $0.10\text{ ms}$) without global re-heapification. Ablation shows the proxemic term is load-bearing: removing it collapses mission success from $100.0\%$ to $11.0\%$.
    \item \textbf{Comprehensive Multi-Domain Empirical Benchmark Suite:} We validate the framework across three distinct architectural topologies (Retail Supermarket, Clinical Hospital, and Airport Terminal) over $4{,}600$ simulation trials in eleven experiments, including a matched-controller baseline that separates routing from vehicle dynamics, a local-social baseline that separates the distributed layer from ordinary human-aware navigation, a route-by-yield factorial, a four-weight sensitivity study on a disjoint seed set, and communication degradation applied where the mesh is causally responsible. Every table and figure in this paper is generated programmatically from the committed raw data by a single analysis pipeline, with no hand-entered numbers; datasets and code are released under open licenses.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work \& Literature Review}
The proposed $\text{D}^2\text{RO}$ framework builds upon foundational work across Multi-Agent Path Finding (MAPF), dynamic graph replanning, local collision avoidance, ad-hoc mesh communication, physical trolley mechatronics, and human-aware navigation.

\subsection{Decentralized and Lifelong MAPF}
Stern \textit{et al.} \cite{stern2019multi} define the classical MAPF problem and its variants, establishing the fundamental vertex and edge conflict models used in grid environments. To address continuous operations, Ma \textit{et al.} \cite{ma2017lifelong} introduced Lifelong Multi-Agent Path Finding for Online Pickup and Delivery (MAPD), where agents dynamically receive tasks without resting.

To overcome the single-point failure risks and communication latency of centralized servers, recent literature has shifted toward decentralized solvers. Dergachev and Yakovlev \cite{dergachev2024decentralized} explored decentralized unlabeled MAPF using target and priority swapping, while Keskin \textit{et al.} \cite{keskin2024negotiation} presented a decentralized MAPF framework utilizing automated negotiation protocols. Furthermore, learning-based approaches have gained traction: Sartoretti \textit{et al.} \cite{sartoretti2019primal} introduced PRIMAL, utilizing reinforcement and imitation learning for decentralized pathfinding, and Skrynnik \textit{et al.} \cite{skrynnik2024learn} developed ``Learn to Follow'' to separate global heuristic sub-goal allocation from low-level local policies. Despite their high scalability, learning-based methods frequently struggle with out-of-distribution environments (e.g., unexpected aisle closures), highlighting the need for search-based dynamic adaptability.

\subsection{Dynamic Replanning in Unknown and Stochastic Environments}
In retail environments, the topological graph changes dynamically as aisles become blocked or crowded. Recalculating full paths from scratch for every agent is computationally prohibitive. Koenig and Likhachev \cite{koenig2002dstar} addressed this with $\text{D}^*$ Lite, an incremental heuristic search algorithm that recalculates only the segments of a path affected by dynamic edge-cost changes.

The application of $\text{D}^*$ Lite to multi-agent systems was successfully demonstrated by Al-Mutib \textit{et al.} \cite{almutib2012dstar}, who utilized it for real-time path planning by treating peer paths as temporary obstacles. Additionally, Wagner and Choset \cite{wagner2011mstar} developed $\text{M}^*$, dynamically varying the dimensionality of the search space only when agent paths conflict. $\text{D}^2\text{RO}$ adapts these incremental principles, allowing trolleys to dynamically inflate traversal costs based on real-time congestion data without recomputing the entire global map.

\subsection{Kinematic Coordination and Local Collision Avoidance}
While MAPF and $\text{D}^*$ Lite provide global waypoints, continuous kinematic control is required for safe micro-maneuvers when agents cross paths. Van den Berg \textit{et al.} \cite{vandenberg2008orca} introduced Optimal Reciprocal Collision Avoidance (ORCA), providing sufficient conditions for multiple robots to avoid collisions in continuous space without explicit communication.

However, standard ORCA suffers from live-locks in narrow, symmetric environments like supermarket aisles, where agents cannot physically pass one another. Dergachev and Yakovlev \cite{dergachev2021distributed} specifically address this, proposing a system that uses continuous reciprocal collision avoidance but falls back on a locally confined MAPF instance when a deadlock is detected. $\text{D}^2\text{RO}$ leverages this exact synthesis: relying on continuous reactive models for open spaces, and spatiotemporal edge reservations for single-file corridors.

\subsection{Multi-Robot Ad-Hoc Communication Protocols}
The transition from centralized control to a truly distributed $\text{D}^2\text{RO}$ system requires robust peer-to-peer communication. Gielis \textit{et al.} \cite{gielis2022codesign} emphasize the critical need for co-designing robotic planning algorithms alongside network constraints.

For decentralized data sharing, robots rely on mobile ad-hoc networks (MANETs). Slyusar and Kulich \cite{slyusar2016manet} evaluated routing protocols for MANETs in multi-robot exploration. Additionally, Loems \cite{edwige2024swarmslam} investigated robot communication within Swarm SLAM, demonstrating how independent agents merge local spatial data over a distributed mesh. In $\text{D}^2\text{RO}$, this translates to an event-driven telemetry protocol where agents broadcast localized edge-cost penalties across a V2V mesh, allowing distant agents to proactively reroute.

\subsection{Indoor Positioning and Physical Hardware Implementation}
Unlike simulated grids, physical shopping trolleys require absolute spatial grounding and customized mechatronics. Zafari \textit{et al.} \cite{zafari2019survey} provide a comprehensive survey of indoor localization technologies, highlighting the superiority of Ultra-Wideband (UWB) and BLE for centimeter-level accuracy in GPS-denied environments. Clark \textit{et al.} \cite{clark2021team} expanded on this with the TEAM framework, demonstrating effective trilateration and mapping utilizing a localized robotic network, while Nugraha \textit{et al.} \cite{nugraha2024ips} proved that fusing Indoor Positioning Systems (IPS) with wheel odometry via Extended Kalman Filters (EKF) drastically reduces navigation drift.

Bringing these concepts into physical retail spaces, Mohamad Azlan \textit{et al.} \cite{azlan2024intcart} developed the \textit{Int-Cart}, an autonomous mobile trolley robot. Their research validates the integration of LiDAR, depth cameras, and DC/BLDC motor controllers into a physical cart chassis, proving the mechanical and sensory viability of deploying autonomous fleets in retail environments.

\subsection{Human-Aware Navigation \& Research Gap}
A consistent theme of the human-aware navigation literature is that robots cannot treat people simply as ``moving cylindrical obstacles''. Kruse \textit{et al.} \cite{kruse2013human} survey this position at length: comfort, legibility and social convention are objectives in their own right, not by-products of collision avoidance, and a planner that satisfies only the geometric constraint can still be experienced as intrusive. Planners must therefore incorporate proxemics (personal-space boundaries) and contextual human activity into routing itself. 

\textbf{The Research Gap:} There remains a distinct lack of hybrid frameworks that fuse proactive, mesh-informed global graph updates with reactive human-aware collision avoidance in highly constrained physical retail spaces. Most decentralized MAPF algorithms assume either complete centralized knowledge (vulnerable to latency/failure) or rely on myopic line-of-sight sensing (resulting in late-stage deadlocks in narrow corridors). The $\text{D}^2\text{RO}$ framework bridges this gap.

\section{Problem Formulation \& The SW-DGO Framework}

\subsection{Topological Roadmap and Kinematic Model}
Let the indoor facility floorplan be represented by an \textbf{undirected physical roadmap} graph $G = (V, E)$, where $V = \{v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n\}$ denotes navigable topological waypoints (e.g., aisle intersections, passing bays, docking bays) and $E \subseteq V \times V$ represents physical corridor edges. From this undirected physical structure, each agent $i$ maintains a \textbf{directed local cost graph} $\vec{G}_i = (V, A_i)$, whose directed arcs $a = (u,v) \in A_i$ carry directional traversal penalties $C_i(u,v,t)$ and directional corridor lock states.

Each autonomous service cart $i \in \{1, \dots, N_{\text{carts}}\}$ is governed by non-holonomic unicycle kinematics:
\begin{align}
\dot{x}_i(t) &= v_i(t) \cos \theta_i(t), \nonumber \\
\dot{y}_i(t) &= v_i(t) \sin \theta_i(t), \quad \dot{\theta}_i(t) = \omega_i(t)
\end{align}
subject to physical actuator bounds $|v_i(t)| \le v_{\max}$ and $|\omega_i(t)| \le \omega_{\max}$.

\subsection{The Distributed Dynamic Edge-Cost Field Formulation}
Rather than relying on centralized graph allocation or isolated myopic sensing, SW-DGO establishes a \textbf{distributed, time-varying edge-cost field} $C_i(u, v, t)$ maintained locally by each autonomous agent $i$. The edge traversal cost $e = (u, v) \in E$ is formalized as:
\begin{multline}
\label{eq:sw_dgo_master}
C_i(u, v, t) = w_D \cdot C_{\text{geom}}(u, v) + w_M \cdot C_{\text{mesh}}(u, v, t) \\
+ w_H \cdot C_{\text{social}}(v, t) + w_S \cdot C_{\text{kinematic}}(i, v, t)
\end{multline}
minimised over paths \emph{subject to} the directional reservation constraint
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reservation_constraint}
e \notin \mathcal{E}^{\text{reserved}}_i(t)
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{E}^{\text{reserved}}_i(t)$ is the set of single-file edges currently
claimed by a peer whose claim precedes agent $i$'s in the total order of
Section~\ref{sec:reservation}.

We state reservation as a \textbf{constraint rather than a weighted term}, and this
is a correction to earlier presentations of this framework, which wrote it as
$w_R \cdot C_{\text{mutex}}$ and described $w_R$ as a fifth tunable weight. That
formulation was not faithful to the mechanism. $C_{\text{mutex}}$ takes only the
values $0$ and $\infty$: a reserved edge is unavailable, and no finite coefficient
makes it more or less unavailable, while an unreserved edge contributes
$w_R \cdot 0 = 0$ for every $w_R$. The coefficient therefore had no operative
magnitude in any reachable state, which is precisely why perturbing it moves no
measured quantity (Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}). Feasibility is the honest
description, and the framework is accordingly \textbf{four weighted soft terms plus
one hard constraint}.

The remaining dimensionless weights $\mathbf{w} = [w_D, w_M, w_H, w_S] =
[1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 1.2]$ are \emph{hand-selected nominal values}, not the output of a
calibration procedure; Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity} reports how the system behaves
when each is perturbed. They balance physical progress against social proxemics,
collaborative telemetry and vehicle clearance:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $w_D = 1.0$: Baseline Euclidean physical distance ($C_{\text{geom}}$ in meters).
    \item $w_M = 1.5$: Weights collaborative V2V congestion alerts $1.5\times$ higher than metric distance, ensuring trailing carts proactively reroute through clear parallel corridors rather than deadheading into blocked aisles.
    \item $w_H = 2.0$: Prioritizes pedestrian personal-space comfort $2.0\times$ above travel distance, enforcing compliant yielding and wide social berths.
    \item $w_S = 1.2$: Kinetic clearance penalty preventing chassis corner scrapes ($1.2\times$ baseline) and anti-tailgating violations.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{1. Intrinsic Metric Geometry \texorpdfstring{$C_{\text{geom}}(u, v)$}{C\_geom(u, v)}}
\begin{equation}
C_{\text{geom}}(u, v) = \|\mathbf{p}_u - \mathbf{p}_v\|_2
\end{equation}

A turn penalty $\alpha_{\text{turn}}|\Delta\theta|$ would be a natural addition here, and earlier presentations of this formulation included one. We have removed it, because charging for a heading change requires the search state to \emph{carry} a heading: the cost of entering an edge then depends on the edge traversed before it, which a position-indexed graph cannot express. Implementing it correctly means augmenting the state space with orientation and re-deriving the incremental repair over that larger space. We record it as future work (Section~\ref{sec:future}) rather than display a term the evaluated system does not contain.

Cornering is not thereby ignored: the motion layer applies a bounded angular rate $\omega_{\max} = 2.50\text{ rad/s}$ and decelerates through turns, so sharp direction changes are penalised in \emph{execution} even though they are not priced in the \emph{search}. The distinction matters for interpreting the results: $\text{D}^2\text{RO}$ does not prefer straighter routes, it merely traverses crooked ones more slowly.

\subsubsection{2. Distributed Anticipatory Cost Propagation \& Horizon Extension \texorpdfstring{$C_{\text{mesh}}(u, v, t)$}{C\_mesh(u, v, t)}}
In decentralized multi-agent systems, myopic line-of-sight sensing restricts an agent's local obstacle horizon to $\mathcal{O}_i^{\text{local}}(t) = \{\mathbf{x} \mid \|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{p}_i(t)\| \le R_s\}$. To eliminate the severe makespan penalty of deadheading into remote blockages, SW-DGO formalizes an 	extbf{effective perception horizon extension}:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{O}_i^{\text{effective}}(t) = \mathcal{O}_i^{\text{local}}(t) \cup \bigcup_{j \in \mathcal{N}_i} \mathcal{O}_j(t - \tau_{ij})
\end{equation}
When agent $j$ discovers a congestion event at edge $(u, v)$ at timestamp $t_k$, it broadcasts a localized event-driven telemetry packet over the ad-hoc mesh with bounded time-to-live ($\text{TTL} = 3$). The received penalty decays exponentially over time:
\begin{equation}
C_{\text{mesh}}(u, v, t) = \sum_{k \in \mathcal{M}(u, v, t)} \gamma_k \cdot \exp\left(-\lambda_{\text{decay}} (t - t_k)\right)
\end{equation}
where $\gamma_k$ is the initial congestion penalty amplitude and $\lambda_{\text{decay}}$ is the temporal decay rate, guaranteeing that stale congestion information naturally attenuates without permanently poisoning the routing topology.

\subsubsection{3. Continuous 2D Asymmetric Human Proxemics \texorpdfstring{$C_{\text{social}}(v, t)$}{C\_social(v, t)}}
To model human psychological personal space (Hall's Proxemics), we formulate an 	extbf{asymmetric directional Gaussian potential field} aligned with pedestrian heading $\theta_h$:
\begin{equation}
H_{\text{prox}}(\mathbf{p}, \mathbf{h}_j, \theta_h) = A_j \cdot \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2} \left[ \left(\frac{x_{\text{local}}}{\sigma_x}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{y_{\text{local}}}{\sigma_y}\right)^2 \right] \right)
\end{equation}
where relative coordinates are transformed into the pedestrian's local body frame:
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix} x_{\text{local}} \\ y_{\text{local}} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta_h & \sin\theta_h \\ -\sin\theta_h & \cos\theta_h \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x - x_j \\ y - y_j \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
with asymmetric longitudinal variance reflecting expanded front personal space:
\begin{equation}
\sigma_x = \begin{cases} 1.35\text{ m}, & x_{\text{local}} \ge 0 \ (\text{front}) \\ 0.60\text{ m}, & x_{\text{local}} < 0 \ (\text{rear}) \end{cases}
\qquad \sigma_y = 0.90\text{ m}
\end{equation}
The continuous social edge penalty is integrated along the edge trajectory segment:
\begin{multline}
C_{\text{social}}(u, v, t) = \int_{e} H_{\text{prox}}(\mathbf{p}, t)\, ds \\
  = \lVert \mathbf{p}_v - \mathbf{p}_u \rVert
    \int_{0}^{1} H_{\text{prox}}\big((1-\tau)\mathbf{p}_u + \tau \mathbf{p}_v, t\big)\, d\tau
\end{multline}

\subsubsection{4. Distributed Directional Bottleneck Reservation \texorpdfstring{$C_{\text{mutex}}(u, v, t)$}{C\_mutex(u, v, t)}}
\label{sec:reservation}

\paragraph{When is a passage single-file?}
Two rectangular carts of width $W_{\text{cart}} = 0.48\text{ m}$ can pass one another
in a straight corridor only if the corridor admits both bodies side by side together
with a wall clearance $c_w$ on each side and a mutual clearance $c_m$ between them:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:singlefile}
W_{\text{corridor}} \;\ge\; 2W_{\text{cart}} + 2c_w + c_m .
\end{equation}
With the clearance margin used throughout this work ($c_w = c_m = 0.54\text{ m}$,
Table~\ref{tab:parameters}) the threshold is $2.58\text{ m}$. For two \emph{discs} of
radius $r$ the corresponding condition is $W \ge 4r$, not $2r$: each centre must stay
$r$ from its wall and the centres must be $2r$ apart. Earlier presentations of this
work stated the condition as $W_{\text{corridor}} < 2r_{\text{safety}}$, which is
geometrically incorrect in either formulation, and we correct it here.

We are also explicit that $0.40\text{ m}$ is an \textbf{effective safety radius},
chosen to bound the swept envelope of a turning cart, and not the inscribed radius of
the $0.72 \times 0.48\text{ m}$ chassis, whose inscribed radius is $0.24\text{ m}$.
The earlier label was simply wrong.

\paragraph{Which edges are designated single-file.}
Applying Eq.~\eqref{eq:singlefile}, the $2.1\text{ m}$ grocery aisles fall below the
$2.58\text{ m}$ threshold and are designated single-file, as are the clinical
corridors; the promenades and concourses are not. We note plainly that a $2.1\text{
m}$ aisle would admit two $0.48\text{ m}$ carts on bare geometry alone --- the
designation follows from the operational clearance the fleet is required to maintain,
and from the fact that retail aisles are routinely occupied by standing shoppers,
end-cap overhang and parked trolleys, not from chassis width in isolation. The
designation is a property of the environment definition and is fixed per edge; it is
not recomputed at run time.

\paragraph{The reservation itself.}
Conflicts in such passages are resolved by a directional reservation tuple:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_e(t) = \langle \text{AgentID}, \vec{d}_{\text{traversal}}, t_{\text{acquire}}, t_{\text{expire}}, \text{Priority} \rangle
\end{equation}
When agent $i$ claims edge $(u, v)$, the reverse edge cost for opposing agents is set to:
\begin{equation}
C_{\text{mutex}}(v, u, t) = \begin{cases} \infty, & (u, v) \text{ reserved by peer } j \neq i \\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
\end{equation}
An opposing agent observes this infinite cost and, because the reservation enters its \emph{graph} rather than its controller, $\text{D}^*$ Lite reroutes it through a parallel aisle or a Turnout Alcove ($V_{\text{alcove}}$) instead of halting at the corridor mouth. Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} shows empirically that this diversion---rather than queueing---is the operative behaviour.

\subsubsection{5. Kinetic Chassis Safety Clearance Envelope \texorpdfstring{$C_{\text{kinematic}}(i, v, t)$}{C\_kinematic(i, v, t)}}
This term has two components, and we write both because the implementation applies
both. A \emph{static} contribution prices proximity to fixed geometry, and a
\emph{dynamic} contribution prices proximity to peer vehicles:
\begin{multline}
C_{\text{kinematic}}(i, \mathbf{p}, t) =
\underbrace{\kappa\big(d_{\text{shelf}} - d(\mathbf{p}, \mathcal{S})\big)_{+}}_{\text{static fixture clearance}} \\
+ \underbrace{\sum_{j \neq i} A_{\text{trolley}} \exp\!\left( -\frac{\|\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{p}_j(t)\|^2}{2\sigma_{\text{trolley}}^2} \right)}_{\text{dynamic peer envelope}}
\end{multline}
where $d(\mathbf{p}, \mathcal{S})$ is the distance from $\mathbf{p}$ to the nearest
shelf face, $(\cdot)_{+}$ denotes the positive part, and $\kappa$ scales the
clearance deficit. The static component enforces the $0.54\text{ m}$ shelf clearance
buffer; the dynamic component enforces the $1.08\text{ m}$ anti-tailgating gap.
Earlier presentations displayed only the peer term, which understated the component
whose ablation produces the largest change in fixture contact
(Section~\ref{sec:tradeoff}).

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Socially-Weighted Distributed Graph Optimization (SW-DGO)}
\label{alg:sw_dgo}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Roadmap graph $G=(V, E)$, Start $s_{\text{start}}$, Goal $s_{\text{goal}}$, V2V Mesh $\mathcal{N}$
\State Initialize local $\text{D}^*$ Lite planner with $G$ and $s_{\text{goal}}$
\State Compute initial shortest path $\Pi \leftarrow \text{ComputeShortestPath}()$
\While{$s_{\text{current}} \neq s_{\text{goal}}$}
    \State $\Delta \mathcal{M} \leftarrow \text{FetchInboundMeshPackets}(\mathcal{N})$
    \State $\text{cost\_changed} \leftarrow \textbf{false}$
    \For{each packet $pkt \in \Delta \mathcal{M}$}
        \State $(u, v) \leftarrow pkt.\text{edge}$
        \State Update edge cost: $c(u, v) \leftarrow C(u, v, t)$ via Eq.~\eqref{eq:sw_dgo_master}
        \State $\text{D*Lite.NotifyEdgeCostChange}(u, v)$
        \State $\text{cost\_changed} \leftarrow \textbf{true}$
    \EndFor
    \State Sample human positions $\{h_j\}$ within radius $R_{\text{sense}} = 240\text{ px}$
    \If{$\text{ProxemicsChanged}(\{h_j\})$}
        \State Update $H_{\text{prox}}(u, v, t)$ for local edges
        \State $\text{cost\_changed} \leftarrow \textbf{true}$
    \EndIf
    \If{$\text{cost\_changed}$}
        \State $\text{D*Lite.ComputeShortestPath}()$
        \State $\Pi \leftarrow \text{D*Lite.ExtractPath}()$
    \EndIf
    \State $s_{\text{next}} \leftarrow \text{GetNextWaypoint}(\Pi)$
    \If{$\text{EdgeIsSingleFile}(s_{\text{current}}, s_{\text{next}})$}
        \State $\text{AcquireCorridorLock}(s_{\text{current}}, s_{\text{next}})$
        \State $\text{Broadcast}(\text{LOCK\_REQUEST}(s_{\text{current}}, s_{\text{next}}))$
    \EndIf
    \State Execute non-holonomic step toward $s_{\text{next}}$ with $S_{\text{trolley}}$ envelope
    \State Decay active mesh penalties: $W_{\text{mesh}} \leftarrow W_{\text{mesh}} \cdot e^{-\lambda \Delta t}$
\EndWhile
\State \textbf{return} Mission Complete
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Comparison with Alternative Simulation Paradigms}
To contextualize the architectural design of $\text{D}^2\text{RO}$, we contrast it with four standard robotics simulation paradigms:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Classical 2D Grid-World MAPF (CBS, PBS, EECBS):} Assumes synchronous discrete time steps and point holonomic agents. Ignores continuous turning kinodynamics, dynamic human loitering, and V2V mesh latency.
    \item \textbf{Pure Continuous Multi-Agent Navigators (ORCA, Social Force):} Operates in continuous velocity space without topological roadmaps. Lacks global topological awareness, so an agent driven into an infeasible velocity state in a narrow corridor has no route-level recourse. Our implementation exhibits this mode (Section~\ref{sec:failure_modes}), though we do not generalise from it to the published algorithm.
    \item \textbf{Network-Centric Packet Simulators (NS-3, OMNeT++):} Models high-fidelity RF propagation and MAC collisions, but lacks robot kinodynamic physics and graph search.
    \item \textbf{3D Rigid-Body Physics Engines (Gazebo, Isaac Sim):} Models full multi-body dynamics, contact mechanics, and sensor ray-tracing. However, executing $N=100$ randomized Monte Carlo trials across multi-agent fleets is computationally prohibitive ($>100\times$ slower than real time).
    \item \textbf{The SW-DGO Hybrid Approach (The Goldilocks Paradigm):} Couples non-holonomic unicycle kinodynamics ($dt = 0.05\text{ s}$, 60 FPS) with continuous Gaussian proxemics, an ad-hoc V2V mesh simulator, and incremental $\text{D}^*$ Lite graph repair, providing deterministic, real-time evaluation ($<0.15\text{ ms}$).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Multi-Domain Simulation Topologies}
The framework is validated across three architecturally divergent environments,
chosen because they stress a social planner in different ways rather than because
they differ cosmetically.

The \textbf{retail supermarket} ($36 \times 24\text{ m}$) is fixture-dense: six
parallel grocery aisles of width $W_{\text{aisle}} = 2.1\text{ m}$, too narrow for
two carts to pass, connected by three transverse promenades (Back Promenade, Action
Alley, Front Concourse) with cashier registers and front-of-store return depots.
Routing here is a choice among a small number of parallel corridors.

The \textbf{clinical hospital} shares those dimensions but adds a priority
structure: Emergency Trauma (ER), sterile operating and MRI suites, inpatient wards,
and designated \textbf{Turnout Alcoves} ($V_{\text{alcove}}$) set into single-file
corridors so that a routine pushchair can pull aside and grant clearance to an
emergency vehicle. Its corridors are the narrowest of the three, but the topology is
strongly channelised, so there is little to decide once a route is committed.

The \textbf{airport terminal} inverts that: a large open departure plaza with
check-in banks, narrow security screening chokepoints, and elongated boarding gate
piers (Gates A1--A4, B1--B4). Open space means many admissible routes, and
Section~\ref{sec:cross_domain_failure} shows this abundance of choice --- not
scarcity --- is what makes it the hardest domain for the planner.

\section{Experimental Results \& Discussion}

\subsection{Experimental Setup \& Randomization Protocol}
All experiments were conducted using the decoupled 2D kinodynamic simulation framework integrating non-holonomic unicycle dynamics ($\Delta t = 0.05\text{ s}$, 20 Hz control loop) across $1200 \times 800\text{ px}$ ($36.0\text{ m} \times 24.0\text{ m}$) floorplans. To ensure statistical rigor and reproducibility:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Random Seed Policy:} Each evaluation executed $N=100$ independent Monte Carlo trials seeded deterministically. Crucially, every planner in a given trial receives the \emph{same} seed, so comparisons are \textbf{seed-paired} rather than independent: the pedestrian crowd a baseline faces is the crowd $\text{D}^2\text{RO}$ faces.
    \item \textbf{Dynamic Pedestrian Behavior:} Pedestrians move with stochastic velocities $v_h \in [0.8, 1.2]\text{ m/s}$, random waypoint heading deflections, and a $30\%$ browsing loitering probability ($p_{\text{loiter}} = 0.30$) generating dynamic Gaussian discomfort Halos.
    \item \textbf{Common Completion Criterion:} Within any given experiment all planners share an identical arrival tolerance of $0.84\text{ m}$ (approximately one cart length) and an identical time budget. An earlier revision applied planner-specific tolerances, scoring the proposed method against a $2.3\times$ more lenient criterion than its baselines; that asymmetry is removed and every result reported here uses the common criterion. Budgets are set well above the longest observed mission for the experiment class so that a timeout indicates genuine non-completion rather than a truncated run: $T_{\max} = 180\text{ s}$ for the benchmark, ablation, cross-domain and crowd-density experiments, $240\text{ s}$ for fleet-size scaling (where queueing grows with fleet size), and $120\text{ s}$ for the two-cart mechanism experiments.
    \item \textbf{Statistical Analysis:} Because the trials are seed-paired and several outcome distributions are zero-inflated and strongly right-skewed, we do not assume normality. Continuous outcomes are compared with the \textbf{Wilcoxon signed-rank} test on within-pair differences, supported by paired bootstrap confidence intervals; binary mission success is compared with \textbf{McNemar's exact} test. $p$-values are \textbf{Holm-adjusted} within each family of comparisons, and every reported $p$ belongs to one specific outcome---no single significance claim is attached to several unrelated metrics. Skewed outcomes are summarised as median [IQR]; symmetric ones as $\mu \pm \sigma$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Physical Insights and Failure Modes Analysis}
\label{sec:failure_modes}

\subsubsection{Artificial Potential Fields: a social failure, not a navigational one}
An earlier revision of this study reported that APF achieves $0.0\%$ mission success and built a local-minima argument upon it. Under the common completion criterion and a time budget calibrated to true mission durations, that result does not reproduce: \textbf{APF completes $100\%$ of its missions} at $34.54 \pm 0.16\text{ s}$. The previous figure was an artefact of a $35.0\text{ s}$ timeout inherited from an earlier, incorrectly scaled kinematic model, not a property of the algorithm. We report the correction explicitly because the conclusion changes: potential fields are not disqualified from these environments by an inability to arrive.

What disqualifies them is \emph{how} they arrive. APF records the highest intimate-space exposure of any planner evaluated---median $204$ ticks [IQR $176$, $276$], against $0$ [$0$, $0$] for $\text{D}^2\text{RO}$ (Wilcoxon signed-rank, $\Delta = -220.7$ [$-239.5$, $-201.4$], Holm-adjusted $p = 2.7 \times 10^{-16}$). A repulsive force that activates only when a pedestrian is already close produces exactly this behaviour: the cart is pushed aside at the last moment, repeatedly, having already entered the person's intimate zone. The deficiency is one of \emph{anticipation}, which is precisely what a cost field evaluated over a planning horizon supplies and a local force law cannot.

\subsubsection{ORCA and Decentralized Local MAPF: results we decline to over-interpret}
Our implementations of Reactive ORCA and Decentralized Local MAPF complete no missions under the common arrival criterion. Instrumented traces show ORCA agents stalled for $91$--$99\%$ of control ticks, having covered only a fraction of a ${\sim}17\text{ m}$ mission, which is consistent with the velocity-obstacle infeasibility mode described in Section~\ref{sec:orca_trap}: in a corridor narrower than twice the safety envelope, the half-plane constraints admit no feasible velocity and the agent halts.

We deliberately stop short of the stronger claim. ORCA is a widely deployed algorithm, and an in-house implementation returning $0\%$ where the published method is used successfully in practice is at least as likely to indicate a defect in our implementation as a fundamental limitation of the method. \textbf{We therefore report these as properties of our implementations, not of the algorithms}, and we identify validation against a reference implementation on a canonical benchmark as required work before any general claim about ORCA's behaviour in single-file corridors can be sustained. The same caution applies to Local MAPF, where traced trials show agents finishing within ${\sim}1.3\text{ m}$ of their goals rather than livelocked---a near-miss, not the permanent token-swapping deadlock previously asserted. Consequently, none of the contributions claimed in this paper rest on the ORCA or MAPF baselines; they rest on the comparison against the matched-controller and unmatched shortest-path arms and APF, all of which complete their missions. For the same reason these two implementations are \textbf{excluded from Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmark}}: plotting them at $0\%$ beside the proposed method would communicate visually a comparison that this paragraph asks the reader not to draw. For completeness, both complete $0.0\%$ [$0.0$, $3.7$] of missions, neither records a deadlock, and their median intimate-space exposure is $229$ [$0$, $766$] ticks for ORCA and $128$ [$123$, $132$] for Local MAPF.

\subsubsection{Anticipatory Horizon Extension vs. Myopic Backtracking}
In decentralized navigation without peer-to-peer telemetry ($W_{\text{mesh}}=0$), trailing carts operate under myopic line-of-sight sensing ($R_s = 7.2\text{ m}$). When an unexpected pedestrian cluster blocks an inner aisle, trailing units travel to the blocked entrance before sensing the obstruction locally, potentially forcing reversal and path re-computation. The V2V mesh is designed to propagate anticipatory cost penalties to upstream agents before they commit to a congested route, enabling topological detours $\Delta T_{\text{anticipation}}$ earlier.

Importantly, the broad Monte Carlo benchmark---where pedestrian blockages and junction opportunities are randomized across $100$ trials---does not consistently instantiate the topology required for this benefit to appear: an upstream agent at a divergence junction while a downstream agent simultaneously encounters a blockage beyond its sensing range. In those trials the mesh pays its overhead (cost-field propagation and broadcast-triggered replanning) without collecting its return, which is why the no-mesh ablation completes the benchmark faster than the full system ($38.23 \pm 16.88\text{ s}$ vs. $47.38 \pm 13.96\text{ s}$; Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). This does not invalidate the mechanism, but neither does the benchmark evidence it: a general-scenario experiment is simply not a test of anticipatory communication. Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} therefore evaluates the mesh under conditions that do instantiate the required topology.

\subsection{Comparative Benchmark: what is attributable to what}
\label{sec:tradeoff}
Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmark} does not show the proposed method dominating on every axis,
and we present it as what it is. No planner recorded a corridor deadlock in any
trial, so that outcome does not separate them and is not discussed further.

\textbf{Social routing works, and the effect is categorical.} Against planners that
do not price personal space, the separation is not marginal: median intimate-space
exposure is $0$ person-seconds [IQR $0$, $0$] for $\text{D}^2\text{RO}$ against
$6.40$ [$6.2$, $6.6$] for both shortest-path arms and $10.18$ [$8.8$, $13.8$] for
APF (Wilcoxon signed-rank, Holm-adjusted $p = 9.4\times10^{-16}$ and
$4.7\times10^{-16}$). Expressed as distinct encounters, the median trial has $0$
inward boundary crossings against $5$ and $8$ respectively.

\textbf{Isolating the planner from the vehicle.} A shortest-path baseline that snaps
its heading at the next waypoint is not a clean comparator for a rate-limited
non-holonomic planner, so we report two: a \emph{matched-controller} arm that is a
$\text{D}^2\text{RO}$ agent with the social terms disabled and its route frozen, and
the conventional unmatched implementation. They differ by only $1.20\text{ s}$
($19.20$ vs.\ $18.00\text{ s}$), so vehicle dynamics account for about $4\%$ of the
$29.18\text{ s}$ separating $\text{D}^2\text{RO}$ from a shortest path. Both record
identical exposure, confirming the matched arm is socially blind as intended.

\textbf{The distributed layer does not pay for itself in this scenario, and we
report that.} Local Social $\text{D}^*$ Lite --- proxemics, yielding, safety and
dynamic replanning, but no V2V mesh and no reservation --- is the comparator a
reader most wants, because it is ordinary human-aware navigation. It achieves
\emph{exactly} the social compliance of the full system (median $0$ person-seconds;
the paired difference is not distinguishable from zero, $p = 1$) at
$39.06 \pm 15.12\text{ s}$ against $47.18 \pm 13.40\text{ s}$, a difference that is
significant ($p = 2.0\times10^{-5}$). In the broad supermarket benchmark the
distributed layer therefore costs roughly $8\text{ s}$ and returns nothing
measurable.

This is a negative result about our own contribution and it bounds the claim
properly. What eliminates intimate-space exposure is the \emph{socially weighted
graph}, which both systems share. What the \emph{distributed} layer adds appears
only where its target topology is instantiated: anticipatory diversion before a
blockage is observable, and corridor conflicts that a single agent cannot resolve
alone. Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} evaluates exactly those conditions, and the
broad benchmark should not be read as evidence for or against mechanisms it does
not exercise.

\textbf{On makespan reporting.} Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmark}(b) summarises makespan over
\emph{successful} missions only, whereas the paired tests operate on all $N=100$
trials including the single $\text{D}^2\text{RO}$ timeout, so the two do not differ
by the same amount. We report both deliberately: a successful-only descriptive
answers ``how long does a completed mission take?'', while the all-trials paired test
answers ``which planner finishes sooner on the same crowd?'', and a timeout is a real
outcome that should not be silently dropped from the second question.

\subsection{Separating Routing from Reactive Yielding}
\label{sec:factorial}
The comparison above still leaves an attribution question. A $\text{D}^2\text{RO}$ agent both routes socially via $H_{\text{prox}}$ and yields reactively to nearby pedestrians, so a difference against a shortest-path baseline could in principle be produced by either. Three conclusions follow from the inferential contrasts:

\textbf{1. $H_{\text{prox}}$ routing produces the social benefit.} With yielding switched off entirely, enabling $H_{\text{prox}}$ routing alone reduces intimate personal-space exposure from $6.45\text{ person-s}$ [IQR $6.2, 6.6$] in Cell~A down to $0.00\text{ person-s}$ [IQR $0.0, 0.0$] in Cell~C (mean difference $-6.40\text{ person-s}$, $95\%$ CI $[-6.48, -6.31]$, $p < 10^{-15}$). Adding reactive yielding to an already social route (Cell~D) leaves median exposure at $0.00\text{ person-s}$ [IQR $0.0, 0.0$]. \textbf{2. Yielding without social routing is a trap (Strong Negative Interaction).} In Cell~B ($H_{\text{prox}}$ OFF, yielding ON), enabling reactive yielding without social routing inflates median intimate exposure to $48.00\text{ person-s}$ [IQR $38.5, 57.8$]: the agent halts beside a pedestrian obstructing its un-weighted shortest path and has no social cost gradient to steer away, so it accumulates prolonged exposure. The interaction contrast $\Delta_{\text{interaction}} = (D - C) - (B - A) = -42.81\text{ person-s}$ ($95\%$ CI $[-48.08, -37.89]$, $p < 10^{-15}$) is strongly negative, demonstrating that reactive yielding is beneficial only when paired with social graph search.

\textbf{3. Social exposure is zero-inflated.} Because exposure distributions are zero-inflated and right-skewed, Cell~C and Cell~D report medians of $0.00\text{ person-s}$ [IQR $0.0, 0.0$], establishing that proxemic routing eliminates intimate-space breaches across the vast majority of trials.

\subsection{Component Ablation Analysis}
The ablation in Table~\ref{tab:ablation} distinguishes the terms of Eq.~\eqref{eq:sw_dgo_master} that are load-bearing in this benchmark from those that are not, and we report both outcomes.

\textbf{The proxemic term carries the framework.} Removing $H_{\text{prox}}$ collapses mission success from $100.0\%$ to $11.0\%$ and raises the median discomfort integral from $0$ [IQR $0$, $0$] to $13.5$ [$11.2$, $15.6$], with makespan degrading from $47.38 \pm 13.96\text{ s}$ to $172.05 \pm 26.67\text{ s}$. Without a social cost the planner routes straight into crowds, then relies on the reactive yielding layer to extricate itself, which it frequently cannot do within the time budget. This single term accounts for most of what distinguishes $\text{D}^2\text{RO}$ from Static $A^*$.

\textbf{The mesh and reservation terms are not exercised by this benchmark.} Removing $W_{\text{mesh}}$ or $R_{\text{lock}}$ leaves success at $100.0\%$ and \emph{reduces} makespan, from $47.38 \pm 13.96\text{ s}$ to $38.23 \pm 16.88\text{ s}$ and $37.86 \pm 15.13\text{ s}$ respectively. The weight-sensitivity study of Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity} corroborates this independently: scaling $w_M$ or $w_R$ by any factor from $0.5$ to $1.5$ changes makespan by $0.0\text{ s}$, because in this scenario the terms they weight are almost never active. Read alone, this ablation argues against both mechanisms. The explanation is that a randomized supermarket scenario rarely instantiates the topology each mechanism addresses---a downstream blockage outside a trailing agent's sensing range, or two agents committed to the same single-file corridor from opposite ends---while their costs (broadcast-triggered replanning, conservative corridor deferral) are paid in every trial. Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} therefore evaluates both under controlled conditions that do instantiate those topologies. We consider it important to report the negative general-scenario result alongside the positive controlled one rather than only the latter.

\textbf{The safety term: planner and controller separated.} Earlier revisions
ablated ``safety'' with a single switch that removed the $w_S$ graph-cost term
\emph{and} the reactive safety controller (clearance bubble, shelf margin, following
gap) at once, so the resulting effect could not be attributed to either. They are
now ablated independently, and the attribution is unambiguous: removing the graph
term alone raises median fixture contacts from $3$ [$2$, $6$] to $5$ [$5$, $8$],
whereas removing the reactive controller alone raises them to $48$ [$35$, $69$], and
removing both to $93.5$ [$82$, $96$].

The effect is therefore mostly the \emph{controller}, not the cost term. Pricing
clearance in the graph helps, but only modestly; what actually keeps the chassis off
the fixtures is the reactive clearance enforcement in the motion layer. We state
this plainly because the previous full-stack ablation invited the opposite reading,
and because it bounds what the $w_S$ term should be credited with.

Counts are reported as median [IQR] rather than $\mu \pm \sigma$: they are
right-skewed enough that the standard deviation exceeds the mean in several
configurations, and a summary such as $5.71 \pm 7.29$ implies negative contacts,
which cannot occur.

\subsection{Cross-Domain Failure Mode Analysis}
\label{sec:cross_domain_failure}
Aim A3 requires that the planner transfer across dissimilar architectures without retuning. Table~\ref{tab:cross_domain} supports this: with identical weights and no domain-specific parameters, $\text{D}^2\text{RO}$ completes $99.0\%$ of missions in the retail supermarket, $100.0\%$ in the clinical hospital and $95.0\%$ in the airport terminal. Generality is therefore demonstrated; what differs across domains is not reliability but the \emph{character} of the routing effort, which the remaining columns expose.

\textbf{The hospital is the easiest domain, not the hardest.} It records the highest success rate and the lowest replan count ($351.9 \pm 66.0$), despite having the narrowest corridors. Its topology is strongly channelised: routes are largely predetermined by the corridor structure, so there are few alternatives to evaluate and little for the planner to reconsider once committed. Its longer mean transit ($31.78 \pm 3.89\text{ s}$) reflects distance and mandatory alcove yields, not indecision.

\textbf{The airport is the hardest, and for an instructive reason.} It requires $989.4 \pm 369.7$ replans---nearly twice the supermarket's $502.3 \pm 155.6$---and is the only domain with non-zero median intimate exposure ($62$ [$18$, $130$], against $0$ [$0$, $0$] in both other domains). An open concourse offers a large, continuously changing set of admissible routes, so a crowd shifting a few metres alters the cost field enough to justify a new plan; the planner spends its effort re-deciding rather than executing. The wide interquartile range indicates the exposure is concentrated in trials where the fleet must cross a dense gate-area crowd, rather than being incurred uniformly. Open topologies thus stress the framework differently from constrained ones---the difficulty is choice abundance, not scarcity---which motivates crowd-density-adaptive scheduling of $w_H$ and $w_M$, and a replan-hysteresis term, as future work.

We note that these figures supersede an earlier revision reporting $80.0\%$ airport success. In that revision the airport scenario reset its random seed to a constant, so its $100$ nominal trials were $100$ repetitions of a single trial; the defect is corrected and the domain now varies per trial as the other two do.

\subsection{Decoupled Scalability Analysis}
\label{sec:scalability}
We vary crowd density and fleet size independently ($N=100$ trials per point) so that the two effects are not confounded. The two dimensions behave very differently, and only one of them is benign.

\input{generated/fig_crowd_density}

\input{generated/fig_fleet_size}

\textbf{Crowd density scales benignly (Fig.~\ref{fig:scalability_crowd}).} Increasing pedestrians from $2$ to $30$ raises the control-step compute time only from $0.093\text{ ms}$ to $0.172\text{ ms}$ and makespan from $30.10\text{ s}$ to $56.30\text{ s}$, while success falls just $100.0\% \to 98.0\%$. Incremental repair is doing its job: a denser crowd perturbs more edges, but $\text{D}^*$ Lite repairs only the affected subgraph, so cost grows with the perturbation rather than with the map. Even at the highest density the controller consumes under $0.4\%$ of the $50\text{ ms}$ budget.

\textbf{A caveat on the latency figures specifically.} Replan latency is the only quantity reported in this paper that is not deterministic. Every other metric---success, makespan, exposure, packet counts---is a property of the seeded simulation and reproduces bit-identically on reruns; latency is wall-clock time and therefore depends on the machine, its load, and the Python interpreter. Regenerating the datasets on a more heavily loaded machine moved these figures by roughly a factor of two while leaving every other number in this paper unchanged. The correct reading is consequently ordinal, not absolute: incremental repair costs a small and slowly growing fraction of the control budget, with ample headroom on commodity hardware. A precise per-platform figure would require a dedicated timing harness on quiesced hardware, which we do not claim here.

\textbf{Fleet size does not (Fig.~\ref{fig:scalability_fleet}), and this is the framework's clearest limitation.} Success holds near $100\%$ up to $8$ carts, then falls to $85.0\%$ at $10$ and $78.0\%$ at $12$, while makespan more than doubles ($50.99\text{ s} \to 110.17\text{ s}$). The mechanism is visible in the mesh column: broadcast volume grows super-linearly, from $8.50 \pm 5.72$ packets at two carts to $1158.60 \pm 336.83$ at twelve, because every agent broadcasts observations that every other agent must incorporate. Beyond roughly eight carts in a $36 \times 24\text{ m}$ floorplan the fleet begins to congest itself: agents increasingly replan around each other's reservations and telemetry rather than around pedestrians.

We state this bound plainly rather than describing the degradation as graceful. For the retail and clinical deployments motivating this work---where a realistic fleet in a single zone is a handful of units---the operating regime sits inside the flat part of the curve. But the present design should not be represented as scaling to large fleets in a confined space, and mesh-traffic suppression (relevance filtering, spatial scoping of broadcasts) is the natural next step for that regime.

\subsection{Mechanism-Specific Validation}
\label{sec:mechanism}
Because the broad Monte Carlo benchmark does not reliably trigger the specific topological conditions under which V2V mesh anticipation and the corridor reservation protocol provide their primary contribution, we designed two controlled experiments that directly exercise each mechanism. These complement the general-scenario evaluation and provide targeted evidence for the core algorithmic novelties.

\subsubsection{Experiment A: V2V Mesh Anticipation ($N=50$ trials)}
Two carts traverse the same aisle system, the leader ahead of the follower, and a pedestrian cluster blocks the leader's forward aisle mid-route. The precondition the implementation actually enforces is stated precisely: a trial is admitted only if \textbf{the blockage lies outside the follower's $7.2\text{ m}$ sensing radius at the moment it is created}, so the follower cannot have perceived it directly. (An earlier description of this experiment claimed the follower begins ``10+ m behind the leader''; that is not the condition the code tests, and the separation between carts is not what makes the trial admissible.) An alternative divergence junction exists upstream of the follower, where it can reroute \emph{before} committing to the congested path. We compare \textbf{Mesh ON} against \textbf{Mesh OFF} on identical seeds.

Two definitions matter for reading Table~\ref{tab:mech_mesh}. \emph{Anticipation lead time} is the interval between the follower's diversion and the moment the blockage would have entered its sensing radius; it is negative when the diversion happens only after contact. \emph{Backtracking distance} is the cumulative increase in Euclidean distance to the goal, i.e.\ ground covered in the wrong direction, rather than a count of re-traversed edges. We also note that sensing here is \textbf{range-limited rather than line-of-sight}: pedestrians are selected by Euclidean distance without ray-casting against shelf occlusion, so the follower's perceptual advantage is if anything overstated relative to a real sensor behind a gondola.

\input{generated/table_mesh_anticipation}

The mechanism behaves as designed. With the mesh enabled the trailing cart diverts $10.70 \pm 4.20\text{ s}$ before it could have observed the obstruction itself, against $-0.10 \pm 0.04\text{ s}$ with the mesh disabled---that is, without telemetry the diversion occurs only \emph{after} contact with the blockage. Backtracking falls from $2.73 \pm 0.87\text{ m}$ to $1.08 \pm 0.68\text{ m}$ and makespan from $23.78 \pm 3.61\text{ s}$ to $21.44 \pm 3.40\text{ s}$ (Wilcoxon signed-rank, Holm-adjusted $p = 3.8 \times 10^{-9}$ and $2.0 \times 10^{-3}$ respectively). Success is $100\%$ in both arms: the mesh does not determine \emph{whether} the mission completes here, it determines how much wasted travel is incurred, which is the claim the mechanism was designed to support. Together with the ablation result of Section~\ref{sec:tradeoff}, the honest summary is that anticipatory telemetry pays when the topology affords an early alternative and costs a little when it does not.

\subsubsection{Experiment B: Directional Corridor Reservation ($N=50$ trials)}
Two carts approach the same single-file corridor from opposite ends, with randomised arrival offsets. The corridor is verified to be genuinely single-file before each trial runs. We compare \textbf{Lock ON} against \textbf{Lock OFF} on identical seeds.

\input{generated/table_corridor_lock}

\textbf{The effect is large.} Mission success rises from $36.0\%$ to $88.0\%$ (McNemar exact, Holm-adjusted $p = 1.7 \times 10^{-4}$) and corridor occupancy falls from $89.41 \pm 42.42\text{ s}$ to $40.01 \pm 30.87\text{ s}$ ($p = 4.8 \times 10^{-3}$). Without the protocol, the majority of trials fail.

\textbf{The mechanism is not mutual exclusion.} A reader who sees ``mutex'' will expect one agent to hold the corridor while the other queues, and that is not what happens. Head-on encounters are statistically indistinguishable between arms ($1.08 \pm 1.18$ with the protocol, $1.00 \pm 0.00$ without; $p = 1$), so the protocol does not prevent the encounter. The deadlock counter registers zero in \emph{both} arms, so the $N_{\text{deadlock}} = 0$ result cannot be credited to it either. And total accumulated waiting is $0.030 \pm 0.070\text{ s}$ against $0.000\text{ s}$ ($p = 0.21$): agents essentially never queue.

\textbf{What happens instead is cost-projected diversion.} When an agent observes a peer's stronger claim, that claim enters its \emph{graph} as an effectively infinite edge cost, and $\text{D}^*$ Lite reroutes through a parallel aisle. The released dataset records this directly. With the protocol enabled, agents occupy $2.16 \pm 1.36$ vertices outside the contested corridor and issue $25.18 \pm 3.22$ route reconsiderations; with it disabled they occupy $0.00$ such vertices and issue $10.16 \pm 4.12$ (Holm-adjusted $p = 2.3 \times 10^{-8}$ and $6.5 \times 10^{-9}$). The corridor is vacated by re-routing, not by turn-taking, which is consistent with occupancy halving while wait time stays at zero.

The head-on encounter still occurs because it is what triggers the exchange of claims; what the protocol changes is the outcome, not the frequency. Correspondingly, the failure mode without it is not a deadlock in the strict sense but a persistent mutual obstruction that expires against the time budget, which is why a counter keyed on unroutable planner states never fires in either arm.

\textbf{We therefore avoid the term ``mutex''} for this component and call it a \emph{directional reservation with cost-projected diversion}. That is a stronger and more general claim than mutual exclusion --- peer intent is converted into a route-level cost that any graph planner can act on --- but it is a different claim, and the stricter computer-science reading of ``mutex'' is not what the evidence supports.

\textbf{A note on the wait metric.} An earlier revision reported $0.00 \pm 0.00\text{ s}$ of waiting and used it as evidence for this interpretation. That figure was not measurable: the per-episode wait timer was reset when the corridor was released, before the experiment read it at end of run, so it would have read zero however long agents actually waited. The value quoted above comes from a separate accumulator that release does not reset. The conclusion survives the correction, but it now rests on a metric that could have contradicted it.

\subsection{Weight Sensitivity}
\label{sec:sensitivity}
The proposed cost function is a weighted sum over four soft terms, so its behaviour could in principle
depend delicately on the four cost weights. If the reported operating point sat on a
narrow ridge, the result would be fragile in a way a reader must be told about. We
therefore perturb each weight in turn by $\times\{0.5, 0.75, 1.25, 1.5\}$ while
holding the other three at nominal, and evaluate on a seed set \textbf{disjoint} from
every other experiment in this paper: the weights were selected by hand, and
assessing their robustness on the seeds used to select and report them would be a
form of tuning on the evaluation set.

\input{generated/fig_weight_sensitivity}

\textbf{The operating point is a plateau, not a ridge.} Mission success remains
between $97\%$ and $100\%$ under every perturbation of every weight. No setting in
the grid produces a collapse, and the nominal configuration is not a special point
on which the method depends.

\textbf{Makespan responds to the social weight in the expected direction, and to
the distance weight less regularly than we previously reported.} Increasing $w_H$,
which prices proximity to people, lengthens missions monotonically
($37.5\text{ s}$ at $\times0.5$ to $47.1\text{ s}$ at $\times1.5$): that is the
distance-versus-sociability dial the formulation is meant to expose, and a
deployment wanting faster, less deferent carts can move along it. The $w_D$
response is not monotone ($52.7$, $40.9$, $46.2$, $46.0$, $40.7\text{ s}$ across the
grid), which we attribute to route switching --- at some weightings the planner
commits to a structurally different corridor rather than the same corridor traversed
at a different price.

An earlier revision reported a clean monotone $w_D$ trend. That result is superseded
and should not be relied upon: it was computed with a heuristic that is inadmissible
for $w_D < 1$ (Section~\ref{sec:admissibility}), so the planner was returning
suboptimal paths in precisely the configurations that anchored the trend.

\textbf{$w_M$ does nothing here, and that is informative.} Scaling the mesh weight by
any factor in the grid changes makespan by $0.0\text{ s}$, corroborating the ablation
and the benchmark: in a randomised supermarket scenario the mesh term is almost never
active, so its coefficient has nothing to scale. The reservation is absent from this
study altogether, because it is a feasibility constraint rather than a weighted term
and has no magnitude to perturb (Section~\ref{sec:reservation}); it is evaluated by
the ON/OFF mechanism experiment instead.

Consistent with this, we describe $[w_D, w_M, w_H, w_S] = [1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 1.2]$
throughout as \textbf{hand-selected nominal weights}. No formal calibration procedure
was performed, and we make no claim that they are optimal --- only that the method's
behaviour is insensitive to their exact values over the range tested.

\subsection{Communication Robustness}
\label{sec:comm}
Every experiment reported above assumes an \textbf{ideal channel}: the V2V mesh
delivers every packet with zero delay. Since the deployment discussion appeals to RF
attenuation in steel-fixture retail environments, that assumption should be tested
rather than left implicit.

We sweep packet loss $\in \{0, 5, 10, 20\}\%$ against one-hop latency $\in \{0, 50,
100, 200\}\text{ ms}$ in the broad scenario, a full factorial of sixteen conditions at
$N=30$ trials each. There, mission success is $100\%$ in every condition and makespan
varies only between $43.6$ and $47.2\text{ s}$ --- a spread far smaller than the
${\sim}16\text{ s}$ standard deviation within any single condition.

That null result is weak evidence, however, and we do not lean on it: the broad
scenario barely uses the mesh, so degrading a channel that carries little traffic
naturally changes little. \textbf{The informative test is to degrade the channel
where the mesh is causally responsible for the outcome}, so we repeat Mechanism~A
(Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}) across a loss $\times$ latency grid. There the picture
is different. Anticipation lead time is essentially unaffected up to $10\%$ loss
($11.01\text{ s}$ clean, $10.00\text{ s}$ at $10\%$ loss with $200\text{ ms}$
latency) but falls sharply at $20\%$ loss, to $7.44\text{ s}$ with no delay and
$6.20\text{ s}$ at $200\text{ ms}$ --- a reduction of roughly $44\%$ in the quantity
the mechanism exists to produce.

The honest statement is therefore bounded: anticipatory rerouting tolerates packet
loss up to about $10\%$ and one-hop delay up to $200\text{ ms}$, and degrades
materially beyond that. Latency alone is well tolerated; loss is what erodes the
advantage, which is consistent with the design, since an alert that is delayed still
arrives while an alert that is dropped does not.

Two caveats bound that claim. First, the mesh contributes little in this scenario to
begin with (Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}), so a result showing that degrading it
changes little is correspondingly weak evidence: the honest reading is that
degradation does no \emph{harm}, not that the protocol would survive degradation in a
scenario where the mesh carried the outcome. Second, the model is a per-hop delay and
an independent drop probability; it does not reproduce bursty loss, correlated
shadowing behind a metal gondola, or contention collapse under load. A physical
deployment would need measurements from the actual RF environment.

We note that this experiment is only meaningful because of a correction made during
this revision. The mesh implementation modelled latency via per-packet delivery
timestamps, but the agent fetched its inbox without supplying the current simulation
time, so every packet was released immediately regardless of its timestamp. Latency
was configurable and inert. The results above were obtained after that path was
repaired.

\section{Conclusion \& Future Research Directions}
This paper introduced the Distributed Dynamic Route Optimization ($\text{D}^2\text{RO}$) framework powered by Socially-Weighted Distributed Graph Optimization (SW-DGO), formalizing a 4-component soft edge traversal cost function $C_i(e, t) = w_D \cdot C_{\text{geom}}(e) + w_M \cdot C_{\text{mesh}}(e, t) + w_H \cdot C_{\text{social}}(e, t) + w_S \cdot C_{\text{kinematic}}(i, e, t)$ minimised subject to a directional corridor reservation constraint ($R_{\text{lock}}$) and solved by incremental $\text{D}^*$ Lite heuristic graph repair. We assess the outcome against the four aims set out in Section~\ref{sec:aims}:

\textbf{A1 (social compliance) is met, and now attributably so.} Median intimate-space exposure is $0.00$ person-seconds [IQR $0$, $0$] against $6.40$ for a matched-controller shortest path and $10.18$ for artificial potential fields ($p < 10^{-15}$). The $2\times2$ factorial of Section~\ref{sec:factorial} establishes that this is produced by the socially weighted graph ($H_{\text{prox}}$) and not by the reactive yielding layer: routing alone drives exposure to $0.00$ person-seconds, while reactive yielding without social routing inflates exposure ($48.00\text{ person-s}$) and produces a strongly negative interaction contrast ($\Delta_{\text{interaction}} = -42.81\text{ person-s}$, $p < 10^{-15}$).

\textbf{A2 (distributed coordination without a central server) is met under targeted conditions.} In the general scenario, the mesh and reservation layer contribute little because their target topologies occur rarely. But where those topologies are instantiated, their contribution is demonstrable: anticipatory rerouting $11.10 \pm 5.93\text{ s}$ before a blockage is observable, and corridor success raised from $36.0\%$ to $88.0\%$ ($p = 1.7 \times 10^{-4}$) via cost-projected diversion.

\textbf{A3 (architectural generality and real-time feasibility) is met within the evaluated family.} The unchanged planner transfers across retail supermarket, clinical hospital and airport terminal topologies ($99.0\%$, $100.0\%$, $95.0\%$ success), with $\text{D}^*$ Lite incremental repairs executing in a median of $0.002\text{ ms}$ (p95 $0.10\text{ ms}$) within a $0.12\text{ ms}$ control step, well inside the $50\text{ ms}$ real-time budget.

\textbf{A4 (attributable evaluation and weight robustness) is met.} By decoupling vehicle kinematics from routing policies, testing a four-weight sensitivity grid on disjoint seeds ($97$--$100\%$ success across all perturbations), and assessing communication degradation where the mesh is causally responsible (holding lead time up to $10\%$ packet loss), we establish that the system's performance is robust and attributable to its core components.

\textbf{A2 (distributed coordination) is met only under the conditions it targets,

\subsection{Theoretical Guarantees \& Their Empirical Limits}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Heuristic Admissibility \& Incremental Optimality:}
    \label{sec:admissibility}
    The heuristic must be a lower bound on true cost. Since every term of
    Eq.~\eqref{eq:sw_dgo_master} after the first is non-negative, a valid bound is
    obtained by retaining only the distance term at its smallest coefficient,
    $h(u, v) = \min(w_D)\,\lVert \mathbf{p}_u - \mathbf{p}_v \rVert$. The scaling is
    not cosmetic: plain Euclidean distance is admissible only while $w_D \ge 1$, and
    at $w_D = 0.5$ an unobstructed edge costs $0.5d$ while the unscaled heuristic
    claims $d$, so $h > c^*$ and the search may return a suboptimal path. Since the
    sensitivity study of Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity} evaluates $w_D$ at $0.5$ and
    $0.75$, the unscaled heuristic was inadmissible for exactly those configurations,
    and results previously reported for them are superseded. With the scaled bound,
    $\text{D}^*$ Lite extracts an optimal path with respect to the currently observed
    cost field while recomputing only the perturbed subgraph \cite{koenig2002dstar}.
    We verify this against fresh Dijkstra solutions over $150$ randomised scenarios
    comprising $750$ successive repairs, \emph{and} across the whole $w_D$ grid, with
    a direct assertion that $h(u, g) \le c^*(u, g)$ at every vertex.
    \item \textbf{Conflict Resolution in Single-File Bottlenecks:} Directional reservations broadcast as \texttt{LOCK\_REQUEST} packets inflate the contested edge cost for the deferring agent, which then diverts through a parallel aisle or holds in a Turnout Alcove ($V_{\text{alcove}}$). We claim empirical conflict \emph{resolution}, not deadlock freedom: no formal guarantee is established here, and the zero deadlock counts we measure occur in both the lock-enabled and lock-disabled arms, so they cannot be attributed to the protocol. A liveness proof under bounded latency and message loss remains open.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations}
We state the principal limitations directly.

\textbf{(i) The pedestrian model is simple and non-reciprocal.} Simulated humans
alternate between walking and browsing, choose stochastic targets and avoid
fixtures, but they do not perceive or react to the robots as social agents: they
neither yield, nor slow, nor adjust their path in anticipation. This study therefore
demonstrates socially weighted robot navigation \emph{around} synthetic moving
pedestrians, not reciprocal human--robot interaction, and the reported exposure
figures should be read as an upper bound on what a real crowd --- which would
partly step aside --- would produce. Stronger claims would require recorded
pedestrian trajectories, a reciprocal social-force model, or human-in-the-loop
trials.

\textbf{(ii) The weights are hand-selected.} Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity} shows the
result is insensitive to them over $\times[0.5, 1.5]$, but no calibration procedure
was performed and we do not claim optimality. The study is also one-at-a-time; joint
or multivariate interactions are untested.

\textbf{(iii) The distributed layer is not shown to be generally beneficial.} It is
neutral-to-costly in the broad benchmark and demonstrably useful only in the targeted
mechanism experiments.

\textbf{(iv) ORCA and Local MAPF are our own implementations} and return $0\%$
success; no claim in this paper depends on them, and they are excluded from the
primary comparison.

\textbf{(v) All results are simulation-based.} No physical fleet was deployed, and
localisation is assumed rather than estimated.

\textbf{(vi) Fleet scaling is bounded} as described above, and \textbf{(vii)} sensing
is range-limited rather than occlusion-aware: pedestrians are selected by Euclidean
distance without ray-casting against shelves, so the perceptual advantage of a
trailing agent is if anything overstated.

\subsection{Considerations for a Physical Deployment}
The evaluation in this paper is entirely simulation-based. The following are therefore \emph{requirements a physical deployment would have to satisfy}, not properties of the implemented system; none of the hardware described below forms part of the evaluated framework.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{RF Signal Attenuation \& Steel Fixture Multipath:} Metallic retail shelf gondolas and merchandise packaging attenuate $2.4\text{ GHz}$ RF signals by $-15\text{ to }-25\text{ dBm}$. A deployment would likely require Sub-GHz ($868/915\text{ MHz}$) or dedicated short-range ($5.9\text{ GHz}$ IEEE 802.11p / DSRC) links with multi-hop TTL forwarding. The exponential penalty decay $W_{\text{mesh}}(t) = W_0 \exp(-\lambda t)$ that \emph{is} implemented and evaluated here provides the relevant safeguard in principle: stale telemetry expires on its own, so intermittent packet loss cannot permanently poison the routing graph. Our mesh model includes latency and loss, but has not been validated against measurements from a real RF environment.
    \item \textbf{Indoor Positioning \& Sensor Fusion:} All results assume the pose is known. A physical cart would need to recover it---plausibly by fusing wheel encoders, an IMU, Ultra-Wideband trilateration and LiDAR scan-matching through an Extended Kalman Filter \cite{nugraha2024ips, zafari2019survey}---and the resulting localisation error would propagate into both the proxemic field and the corridor reservations. Quantifying that sensitivity is future work.
    \item \textbf{Payload Invariance \& Non-Holonomic Kinodynamics:} Service carts undergo large payload shifts (on the order of $15\text{ kg}$ empty to $65\text{ kg}$ loaded), altering the centre of gravity and rotational inertia. The evaluated system uses fixed kinodynamic limits; a deployment would need to modulate $a_{\max}$ and $\kappa_{\max}$ from load sensing to avoid wheel scrubbing or tip-over. This is not implemented or evaluated here.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Future Research Directions}
\label{sec:future}
The limitations above define the immediate agenda; the last two items are longer-range.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Joint Weight Calibration:} Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity} perturbs each weight one at a time and finds a plateau, but says nothing about interactions between them. Future work should treat calibration as a multi-objective problem --- a Pareto front over makespan and social exposure, task-dependent weightings, or automatic selection from a target compliance level --- rather than a one-at-a-time sweep.
    \item \textbf{Baseline Validation:} Reproducing our ORCA implementation's behaviour against a reference (e.g. RVO2) on a canonical benchmark, so that comparative claims rest on validated baselines.
    \item \textbf{Mesh Traffic Suppression:} Relevance filtering and spatial scoping of broadcasts, to lift the fleet-size ceiling identified in Section~\ref{sec:scalability}.
    \item \textbf{Heading-Augmented Search:} Implementing $\alpha_{\text{turn}}$ over a state space augmented with heading, allowing turn cost to enter the graph search rather than only the motion layer.
    \item \textbf{Hybrid Learning-Guided Search:} Coupling Graph Neural Networks or deep reinforcement learning (e.g., PRIMAL / Learn to Follow) for global sub-goal allocation with the deterministic $\text{D}^2\text{RO}$ engine for kinodynamic execution.
    \item \textbf{Heterogeneous Multi-Agent Ecosystems:} Extending SW-DGO to mixed fleets---delivery pods, floor-scrubbing AGVs, human-operated wheelchairs---via vehicle-specific agility weights in the $R_{\text{lock}}$ reservation tuple.
\end{enumerate}
